Advanced SQL Analytics

Same data warehouse as the SQL EDA project, one level deeper: window functions, segmentation, and year-over-year performance, told business-first.

Why go past exploration

The SQL EDA project answers "what happened." This one answers "how much of it matters, and is it repeatable." Same gold schema, PostgreSQL 17, but now using window functions, CTEs, and two reporting views (gold.report_customers, gold.report_products) to get comparisons a plain GROUP BY can't give.

Does the Bikes concentration hold up under a stricter method?

The EDA project found Bikes at 96.5% of revenue with a simple GROUP BY. Here I recompute the same share with a window function, so the category total and the grand total live in the same row, no separate query to cross-check against.

Query

WITH category_sales AS (
    SELECT p.category, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_sales
    FROM gold.fact_sales f
    LEFT JOIN gold.dim_products p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
    GROUP BY p.category
)
SELECT category, total_sales,
    SUM(total_sales) OVER () AS overall_sales,
    ROUND((total_sales::numeric / SUM(total_sales) OVER ()) * 100, 2) AS percentage_of_total
FROM category_sales ORDER BY total_sales DESC;

96.46%, same conclusion, independently verified. That consistency is the point: two different techniques agreeing means the concentration risk isn't a query artifact, it's real, and it belongs in front of whoever owns the product roadmap.

What does the product catalog actually cost to stock?

Segmenting products into cost bands turns 295 individual price tags into a shape I can act on: is this a catalog of cheap accessories with a few flagship items, or something more evenly spread?

Query

SELECT
    CASE
        WHEN cost < 100 THEN 'Below 100'
        WHEN cost BETWEEN 100 AND 500 THEN '100-500'
        WHEN cost BETWEEN 500 AND 1000 THEN '500-1000'
        ELSE 'Above 1000'
    END AS cost_range,
    COUNT(product_key) AS total_products
FROM gold.dim_products
GROUP BY cost_range ORDER BY total_products DESC;

71% of the catalog (211 of 295 products) costs under $500. Only 39 products sit above $1,000. Combined with the category finding above, the picture is a business selling a lot of accessory-priced items and a small set of premium bikes — the accessory volume just isn't converting into accessory revenue.

Was the 2013 sales spike real growth?

The EDA project flagged 2013 nearly tripling 2012. Year-over-year performance at the product level is what tells me if that was broad, sustainable growth or a handful of one-off events.

Query

WITH yearly_product_sales AS (
    SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM f.order_date)::int AS order_year,
        p.product_name, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS current_sales
    FROM gold.fact_sales f
    LEFT JOIN gold.dim_products p ON f.product_key = p.product_key
    GROUP BY EXTRACT(YEAR FROM f.order_date), p.product_name
)
SELECT order_year, product_name, current_sales,
    LAG(current_sales) OVER (PARTITION BY product_name ORDER BY order_year) AS py_sales
FROM yearly_product_sales ORDER BY product_name, order_year;

This pattern (159 in 2012, 37,683 in 2013, back down to 1,749 in 2014) repeats across dozens of products, not just one. That's not organic growth, that's a spike and a crash. Whatever drove 2013 — a bulk order, a promotion, a channel that later closed — it wasn't durable, and treating it as the new baseline would be a planning mistake.
