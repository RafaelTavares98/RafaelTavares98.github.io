SQL EDA — Notebook draft

Draft layout. Same PostgreSQL analysis as the SQL EDA project, walked through cell by cell: the question, the query, the result, and what it means for the business.

Cell 1 — Business question

How healthy is the business, at a glance?

SELECT 'Total Sales' AS measure_name, SUM(sales_amount) AS measure_value FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Total Quantity', SUM(quantity) FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Average Price', AVG(price) FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Total Orders', COUNT(DISTINCT order_number) FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Total Products', COUNT(DISTINCT product_name) FROM gold.dim_products
UNION ALL
SELECT 'Total Customers', COUNT(customer_key) FROM gold.dim_customers;
MeasureValue

Total quantity60,423
Average price486.04
Total orders27,659

Decision: Before slicing anything, get the one-screen number set a stakeholder would ask for first. Average order size lands near $1,061 (29.36M / 27,659 orders), which sets the baseline every later cut gets compared against.

Cell 2 — Business question

Which product category is actually paying the bills?

SELECT p.category, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_revenue
FROM gold.fact_sales f
LEFT JOIN gold.dim_products p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;
CategoryRevenueShare
Bikes28,316,27296.5%
Accessories700,2622.4%
Clothing339,7161.2%
Decision: 96.5% of revenue rides on one category. That is not a strength, it is concentration risk: if Bikes has one bad quarter, the whole business has a bad quarter. The pragmatic move is not "sell more bikes," it is asking whether Accessories and Clothing are underinvested or just genuinely low-demand — worth a follow-up cut by margin, not just revenue.

Cell 3 — Business question

Is the business growing or shrinking, year over year?

SELECT
    DATE_TRUNC('year', order_date) AS order_date,
    SUM(sales_amount) AS total_sales,
    SUM(SUM(sales_amount)) OVER (ORDER BY DATE_TRUNC('year', order_date)) AS running_total_sales
FROM gold.fact_sales
WHERE order_date IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY DATE_TRUNC('year', order_date);
YearSales
2010 (partial, Dec only)43,419
20117,075,088
20125,842,231
201316,344,878
2014 (partial, Jan only)45,642
Decision: 2012 dipped 17% against 2011, then 2013 nearly tripled it. A single trend line would hide that dip. Before calling 2013 a win, the real next question is what changed operationally between 2012 and 2013 — new product line, new region, or a pricing change — because that is the repeatable part, not the number itself.

Cell 4 — Business question

Who are the customers worth protecting?

SELECT c.customer_key, c.first_name, c.last_name, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_revenue
FROM gold.fact_sales f
LEFT JOIN gold.dim_customers c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key
GROUP BY c.customer_key, c.first_name, c.last_name
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC
LIMIT 10;

Decision: The top 10 customers cluster tightly around ~13,000 each, no single whale. That is good news for retention risk (losing one customer will not sink the business) but it means a loyalty program needs to target a band, not a handful of VIP names.

Cell 5 — Business question

What does the customer base actually look like?

-- VIP: 12+ months history, spend > 5,000
-- Regular: 12+ months history, spend <= 5,000
-- New: less than 12 months history
SELECT customer_segment, COUNT(customer_key) AS total_customers
FROM segmented_customers
GROUP BY customer_segment
ORDER BY total_customers DESC;
SegmentCustomers
New14,631
Regular2,198
VIP1,655
Decision: 79% of the customer base is "New" — under 12 months of history. That is not automatically a growth story; it could just as easily mean weak retention past year one. The next query this should force is a cohort retention check, not another acquisition metric.
