SQL EDA — Notebook draft

Exploratory data analysis on a sales data warehouse, told business-first: the question, the query, and the decision it drives.

Why this data set, and why this way

This is the same gold schema as the SQL EDA project: 18,484 customers, 295 products, 60,398 sales records, loaded into PostgreSQL 17. The original project runs the 13 exploration scripts top to bottom. This page picks 5 of those results and presents them the way I'd actually walk a stakeholder through them: what did I ask, what came back, and what does it change about how we run the business. No result gets a number without a decision attached to it.

How healthy is the business, at a glance?

Before slicing anything, I pull the one-screen number set a stakeholder would ask for first: total sales, orders, products, and customers. It sets the baseline everything else gets compared against.

Query

SELECT 'Total Sales', SUM(sales_amount) FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL SELECT 'Total Orders', COUNT(DISTINCT order_number) FROM gold.fact_sales
UNION ALL SELECT 'Total Customers', COUNT(customer_key) FROM gold.dim_customers;

Average order size lands near $1,061 (29.36M across 27,659 orders). That's the number I'll keep coming back to as a sanity check on everything downstream.

Which product category is actually paying the bills?

Splitting revenue by category is usually the first real business cut, not a technical exercise. It answers "where does the money actually come from."

Query

SELECT p.category, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_revenue
FROM gold.fact_sales f
LEFT JOIN gold.dim_products p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
GROUP BY p.category ORDER BY total_revenue DESC;

96.5% of revenue rides on Bikes alone. That's not a strength, it's concentration risk — one bad quarter for Bikes is a bad quarter for the whole business. The pragmatic follow-up isn't "sell more bikes," it's checking whether Accessories and Clothing are underinvested or genuinely low-demand, ideally cut by margin next, not just revenue.

Is the business growing or shrinking, year over year?

A single trend line hides more than it shows. Breaking sales out by year is what actually surfaces whether growth is real or a one-off spike.

Query

SELECT DATE_TRUNC('year', order_date) AS order_date, SUM(sales_amount) AS total_sales
FROM gold.fact_sales
WHERE order_date IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY DATE_TRUNC('year', order_date);

2012 dipped 17% against 2011, then 2013 nearly tripled it. Before calling 2013 a win, the real next question is what changed operationally between the two years — new product line, new region, pricing — because that's the repeatable part, not the number itself.

Who are the customers actually worth protecting?

Ranking customers by revenue tells me whether the business depends on a handful of whales or a broader base, which changes how a retention program should be built.

Query

SELECT c.customer_key, c.first_name, c.last_name, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_revenue
FROM gold.fact_sales f
LEFT JOIN gold.dim_customers c ON c.customer_key = f.customer_key
GROUP BY c.customer_key, c.first_name, c.last_name
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC LIMIT 10;

The top 10 cluster tightly around ~13,000 each. No single whale. Good news for retention risk, but it means a loyalty program needs to target a band of similar customers, not chase a handful of VIP names.

What does the customer base actually look like?

Segmenting by lifespan and spend (VIP, Regular, New) turns a flat customer list into something a marketing or CS team can act on.

Query

-- VIP: 12+ months history, spend > 5,000
-- Regular: 12+ months history, spend <= 5,000
-- New: less than 12 months history
SELECT customer_segment, COUNT(customer_key) AS total_customers
FROM segmented_customers GROUP BY customer_segment;

79% of the customer base is "New," under 12 months of history. That isn't automatically a growth story, it could just as easily be weak retention past year one. The next query this forces is a cohort retention check, not another acquisition metric.
